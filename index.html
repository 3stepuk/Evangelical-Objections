Thinking it through

Nine Objections — and the Catholic Answer

The things an evangelical friend will actually say, stated fairly, and answered from Scripture and the early Church.

How to read this

These are the nine objections a serious evangelical raises. I have tried to state each one in its strongest form, because a weak version is no use to anyone.

Where the answer is contested, I say so. Where the Catholic claim is misunderstood, I say that too, because half of these arguments fail on a mishearing rather than on the merits.

Who is writing this

I am not a Catholic. Not yet, and maybe not ever, because that is not a thing anyone can promise in advance.

I was converted at eighteen, in the evangelical world, and I meant it. I went to Bible college. I ministered in a charismatic church. I would have called myself a Calvinist, and I believed in faith alone, grace alone and Scripture alone. I could have argued the case from memory, with the proof texts in order.

What changed was not an argument I lost. It was reading the first two centuries and finding that the men who came immediately after the apostles did not sound like the people who taught me.

So this is not a convert's brief. It is what an inquirer found when he started checking the things he had been certain of.

I still hold much of what I was taught. I also think some of it was given to me as history when it was not history at all.

One: “the Catholic Church went astray early”

The claim: the Church became apostate within a few centuries. Baptismal regeneration is dated to the fourth century, the Mass to the sixth, and the true faith went underground until the Reformation.

The answer: the dates are simply wrong, and the evidence is older than the claim.

The Didache, written around the year 90, describes the Eucharist as a sacrifice. Ignatius of Antioch, writing about 110 on his way to martyrdom, calls it the flesh of Christ. Justin Martyr, around 150, describes baptism as new birth and the Eucharist as the body and blood.

That is within one lifetime of the apostles. These are not medieval inventions. They are the men who were taught by the apostles or by their immediate successors.

And the promise matters. Christ said the gates of hell would not prevail against his Church, and that he would be with it always. A Church that vanished for fifteen hundred years is hard to square with either.

Two: “the Church has two heads”

The claim: Catholics put the Pope alongside Christ. Baptism is confused with the Spirit. And the Catholic rejection of eternal security denies the assurance of salvation.

The answer: the Pope is a steward, not a rival. Christ is the head of the Church. The Pope is his vicar, which is the office of a prime minister, not a second king.

The pattern is in Isaiah 22, where the king gives a steward the keys of the house of David. Christ gives those same keys to Peter in Matthew 16. A steward with keys is not a second monarch, and everyone in the first century knew it.

On baptism, Scripture does not divide water from the Spirit. Jesus says unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom. The division is a later idea, not a biblical one.

On assurance, the Catholic position is not despair. The Church teaches that a person living in grace can have moral certainty of salvation. What it condemns is presumption: the claim of guaranteed salvation regardless of what one does next. Paul himself says he disciplines his body lest, after preaching to others, he should be disqualified.

Three: “Scripture alone is the authority”

The claim: Catholics put tradition above the Bible. The Bible teaches that Scripture alone is the final authority.

The answer: the Bible does not teach that, and it could not have.

The phrase is not in Scripture. What is there is 2 Thessalonians 2:15, where Paul commands the Church to hold fast to the traditions taught, whether by word or by letter. And 1 Timothy 3:15 calls the Church the pillar and foundation of the truth.

Then there is the practical problem. The Bible did not arrive with a table of contents. The list of books was settled by councils of the Church in the fourth century, at Rome, Hippo and Carthage. If you trust the Bible, you are trusting the body that told you which books belong in it.

That argument is not decisive on its own, and honest Protestants say so. But it does mean that "the Bible alone" is a claim the Bible itself does not make.

Four: “the Mass sacrifices Christ again”

The claim: the Mass re-sacrifices Jesus, which denies Hebrews 10 and his words, "It is finished." And a crucifix shows a dead Christ, which denies the resurrection.

The answer: the Church condemns the idea of re-sacrifice as firmly as any Protestant. The Catechism says plainly that Christ's sacrifice is unique and unrepeatable.

What the Mass does is make that one sacrifice present. Christ is not killed again. He is eternally the same person, outside time, and his one offering on Calvary is eternally present to the Father. The Mass is an anamnesis, a making present, not a repetition.

Hebrews says Christ holds his priesthood permanently. What does a priest do? He offers sacrifice. Revelation shows him as a lamb standing, as though slain. The Mass is our share in that heavenly liturgy.

On the crucifix: Paul says he decided to know nothing but Jesus Christ and him crucified. A crucifix is obedience to that sentence, not a denial of Easter.

Five: “a different gospel — salvation by works”

The claim: Catholicism teaches grace plus merit, which is the error Paul condemns in Galatians.

The answer: the Church condemned that error first. The Council of Trent anathematised the idea that anyone can be saved by his own works without grace. It is not a permitted opinion in Catholic teaching; it is a heresy the Church named, Pelagianism.

The confusion is usually about what justification is. Protestants often describe it as a legal declaration: God covers the sinner but leaves him unchanged. Catholics describe it as a real change: grace actually makes the person holy.

The "plus works" that gets attacked is, on the Catholic account, Christ's own life working in the believer. Paul says it himself: I live, yet not I, but Christ lives in me.

And on the phrase itself: "faith alone" appears once in the Bible, in James 2:24, and it appears in order to deny it.

Six: “you cannot earn a gift”

The claim: if you have to do anything, it is not a gift.

The answer: this is the strongest form of the objection, and it deserves a straight answer.

Catholics do not say grace is earned. They say grace is received, and that receiving is not passive. A man dying of thirst does not earn the water by drinking it. Faith that does nothing is, on James's account, not faith at all but a claim to faith.

Seven: “Mary is made a mediator”

The claim: calling Mary mediatrix robs Christ of his unique mediation, since there is one mediator between God and man.

The answer: the same passage settles it. Four verses before 1 Timothy 2:5, Paul urges Christians to make intercessions for one another. If asking a friend to pray for you does not rival Christ's mediation, neither does asking his mother.

Catholic teaching is that Mary's mediation is entirely subordinate and entirely his. She has no light of her own. The old image is the moon: it shines only because of the sun.

EIGHT: "MORTAL AND VENIAL SIN IS AN INVENTION - AND PURGATORY UNDERMINES THE CROSS"

Eight: “Mortal and venial sin is an invention — and purgatory undermines the cross”

The claim: dividing sins into categories repeats the serpent's suggestion that some disobedience is harmless. And purgatory is a second chance, which makes the cross insufficient.

The answer: the distinction is in the Bible. First John 5 speaks of sin that leads to death and sin that does not, and tells Christians to pray for the second kind.

Purgatory is not a second chance. It is for those already saved. Revelation says nothing unclean enters heaven. If a Christian dies in grace but still clings to resentment or pettiness, God's mercy finishes the work. Paul describes it exactly: a man whose work is burned up will suffer loss, yet he himself will be saved, but only as through fire.

That is not a denial of the cross. It is the cross applied.

Nine: “the sacraments are a hamster wheel”

The claim: the Catholic system is a cycle of rituals with no assurance, and it puts people on the broad road.

The answer: Christ said his flesh is true food, and unless you eat it you have no life in you. He also said that if we love him we will keep his commandments. Neither sentence is a wheel. Both are the means by which grace is actually delivered to people with bodies.

What this page is not

This is not an argument that Catholics are right about everything by default, and it is not a claim that every Protestant is dishonest. Most of these objections come from people who love Christ and have been told a version of Catholicism that is not what the Church teaches.

The best outcome of reading this is not winning. It is wanting to check. That is what happened to me, and I am still checking.
